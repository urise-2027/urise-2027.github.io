Opening Keynote Address · Literature: The Silent Voice of History
Prof. Dr. Asghar Nadeem Syed
Keynote speaker and title confirmed
View provisional abstract

History frequently records institutions, political events, and public figures while leaving ordinary lives, private suffering, and marginalized experience at the edge of the archive. This keynote considers literature as a silent yet enduring voice through which such absences become legible. Focusing on fiction, drama, poetry, and cultural narrative, it examines how writers preserve emotional truth, social conflict, memory, and dissent beyond the limits of official historiography. Literary texts do not merely illustrate the past; they interpret the structures of power that determine whose stories are remembered and whose are suppressed. With particular reference to Pakistan and South Asia, the address reflects on how storytelling responds to violence, inequality, migration, changing cultural values, and the pressures of public life. It argues that literature functions as a counter-archive: a space where forgotten histories speak, collective conscience is tested, and societies encounter the human consequences of their historical choices.

Dr. Subbah Mir · Fulbright alumna; Assistant Professor, Department of English, Forman Christian College (A Chartered University). Her teaching, research and community-service experience spans national and international contexts, with research interests in modern drama, activist theatre, transculturality, social psychology and community development.
Featured speaker confirmed · scheduled immediately after the roundtable
Technical note: Multimedia projector requested.
View provisional abstract

This featured talk examines theatre as a living form of cultural expression and a public practice of dialogue in contemporary Pakistan. It explores how performance brings language, body, space, music, and collective memory together to interpret questions of identity, inequality, belonging, and social change. Particular attention is given to theatre's relationship with activism and community engagement: the ways performance can make overlooked experiences visible, create spaces for participation, and invite audiences to reconsider familiar narratives. At the same time, the talk addresses the artistic and ethical challenges of representing communities, negotiating institutional constraints, and balancing aesthetic experimentation with social purpose. Through examples from performance practice and cultural work, it argues that theatre does not simply reflect society; it actively produces encounters in which meanings are contested and new forms of solidarity may emerge. The session positions the stage as both an artistic space and a civic forum.

Love: The Stories That Shape Us
Abdullah Farhatullah · folk songs, Sassi-Punnu and Sohni-Mahiwal
Venue: Hakeem Saeed Hall · Parallel session
Session confirmed
View session details

Have you ever wondered whether the way you love is truly your own—or whether you learned it from the stories you grew up hearing?

Long before we fall in love, we have already heard stories about love. Stories of lovers who defy their families, sacrifice everything for one another, and choose death over separation. Through poetry, music, and folklore, generations have passed down not just stories of love, but ideas about what love should be.

This talk explores how our culture, particularly our folk songs, shapes our understanding of love. Through selected songs inspired by the legendary tales of Sassi-Punnu and Sohni-Mahiwal, the speaker examines how these stories have influenced our ideals of romance, devotion, sacrifice, and longing across generations.

Do we fall in love on our own terms, or have generations of stories already taught us what love should look like?

Language, Identity, and Cultural Expression in a Globalized World: A Sociolinguistic Perspective

This keynote examines language as both a communicative resource and a central medium through which cultural identity is performed, negotiated, and transformed. Drawing on sociolinguistics, World Englishes, language policy, semiotics, and intercultural communication, it considers how multilingual speakers mobilize linguistic varieties, accents, symbols, and cultural references across local and global settings. Particular attention is given to educational institutions, digital platforms, migration, and creative expression as spaces where identities are made visible and contested. The address argues that globalization does not simply erase linguistic difference; it produces new forms of hybridity, code choice, and cultural affiliation. By connecting linguistic practice with literature, media, music, and everyday representation, the keynote highlights the need for inclusive language policies that recognize multilingualism as cultural knowledge and enable communities to participate in global exchange without surrendering their distinctive voices.

International Keynote Address · The Migration of Cultural Heritage along the Silk Road: Reimagining Uzbekistan's Cultural Identity and International Image
Dr. Guzal Kambarova · Institute of Art Studies, Academy of Sciences of Uzbekistan
Keynote scheduled
View abstract

Since independence in 1991, Uzbekistan has used cultural heritage, especially traditional crafts, to rebuild its national identity and international image. Rooted in the Silk Road's history of exchange, this policy combines heritage protection, support for artisans, national museums, and international festivals. Recent recognition of Uzbek cities as World Craft Cities, and of Bukhara by UNESCO as a Creative City, strengthens national branding and craft tourism. The paper also asks whether a shared Silk Road heritage can ease symbolic competition in Central Asia and complement national identities.

Digital Storytelling and Cultural Memory: Communicating National Identity through Film and Media

Digital media have become powerful sites for preserving cultural memory and communicating national identity to local and international audiences. This keynote explores how film, documentary practice, short-form media, and institutional communication translate historical experience into emotionally resonant public narratives. It considers the creative and ethical choices involved in selecting images, voices, music, and symbols, as well as the tension between cultural authenticity and the demands of global visibility. Drawing on communication education, public diplomacy, and the production context of The Dance of Triumph, the address examines how audiovisual storytelling can connect personal testimony with collective memory and encourage intercultural understanding. It argues that effective cultural communication requires more than promotion: it depends on responsible representation, media literacy, contextual knowledge, and collaboration with the communities whose experiences are being narrated. The keynote concludes by identifying practical principles for culturally grounded storytelling in contemporary digital environments.

Keynote · Surviving AI: Lessons from Folk Literature
Dr. Muhammad Sheeraz Dasti
Speaker confirmed
View provisional abstract

As artificial intelligence reshapes knowledge, creativity, labour, and communication, folk literature offers an unexpected but valuable ethical resource. This keynote reads folktales, qissas, proverbs, songs, and oral narratives as repositories of collective intelligence developed through generations of human experience. Such traditions repeatedly address questions now intensified by AI: the limits of power, the danger of deceptive appearances, the value of practical wisdom, the consequences of desire, and the responsibility that accompanies knowledge. The address argues that survival in an AI-mediated world requires more than technical competence. It also requires narrative judgment, cultural memory, empathy, and the capacity to recognize moral complexity. Bringing folk wisdom into conversation with digital humanities and contemporary technology, the keynote proposes that preservation and digitization should not reduce oral heritage to data alone. Instead, these traditions can help communities evaluate technological change while retaining linguistic diversity, human agency, and culturally grounded ways of knowing.

Khalid Hasan Khan is a producing graduate of New York Film Academy, Los Angeles, United States, and an award-winning filmmaker with over 26 international awards and more than 100 international film-festival screenings worldwide. Known for his concept-driven storytelling and genre-defying approach, Khan’s work spans psychological thrillers, social documentaries, environmental filmmaking and organic narratives. His cinematic vision bridges classical storytelling traditions with emerging technologies, creating films that are intellectually engaging, socially relevant and globally resonant. His diverse filmography includes Hotal, his double award-winning debut psychological thriller; The Unmasking, exploring vaccine hesitancy; The Painterist, examining intellectual piracy; Twinkling Without Shining, documenting the lives of Afghan refugee children; Barefoot, an interfaith sports documentary; Murder of Mystic, an environmental documentary; and Time Sentence, an AI-driven courtroom drama. His work is united by an enduring commitment to originality, experimentation and stories that transcend geographical and cultural boundaries. His recent works include Screen Locked, an award-winning techno-thriller on digital addiction; Vellichor, an upcoming literary thriller; and Fury on the Shortwave, a forthcoming anti-war thriller.

Acting for Life
Abdullah Farhatullah
Session confirmed
View session details

What if acting is not just a profession, but a skill we use every single day to survive?

We may not always realise it, but life is a stage on which we are constantly performing. We play different roles, adapt to changing circumstances, conceal our fears, negotiate expectations, and sometimes become someone the situation demands us to be.

The speaker, an actor by profession, believes that acting is one of the most essential tools for survival. But acting goes far beyond fancy costumes, elaborate make-up, and rehearsed lines delivered in front of a camera or on a stage. At its heart, acting is about understanding people, responding to circumstances, and transforming ourselves to meet the demands of the moment.

Through Acting for Life, the spotlight shifts from the stage to everyday life, inviting us to look at the ordinary human being and discover the extraordinary performer within each of us. How do we behave when circumstances change? What roles do we assume to navigate relationships, challenges, and expectations? And how often do we act without even realising that we are doing so?

Perhaps acting is not something we switch on when we step onto a stage. Perhaps it is something we have been practising all our lives.

International Keynote · Gender, Identity and Cultural Representation (working theme)
Prof. Dr. Khanum Shaikh
Keynote speaker confirmed · title remains provisional
View working title and provisional abstract

Gender, Identity, and Cultural Representation across Transnational Muslim Worlds

This keynote explores how gendered identities are represented and negotiated across transnational Muslim communities through literature, dress, media, religious discourse, and everyday cultural practice. It considers the ways migration, race, class, faith, and national belonging shape the public visibility of Muslim women and complicate simplified narratives of agency or oppression. Using an interdisciplinary feminist framework, the address examines how cultural texts and embodied practices can function simultaneously as sites of regulation, memory, resistance, and self-fashioning. It also asks how representations produced within academic, media, and popular contexts travel across borders and influence public understanding of Muslim lives. By foregrounding situated voices and historical context, the keynote advocates approaches that recognize difference within Muslim communities while remaining attentive to unequal structures of power. The discussion connects gender scholarship with the symposium's wider concerns regarding texts, textures, identity, performance, and cultural expression.
